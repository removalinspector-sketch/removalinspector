How to read a South African credit report

A credit report contains several kinds of information. The score is only one part. To understand what a lender or other permitted user may see, inspect the underlying entries, dates, account statuses and sources.

Reviewed 19 November 2026 · General educational information

Begin with identity information

Check your name and identifying details against your own records. If an account or enquiry appears to belong to someone else, note the exact entry and ask the bureau to investigate the identity match. Do not assume that a similar name means the account is yours. Follow the bureau's official secure verification process and provide only the information it reasonably requests.

Review accounts one at a time

For each account, compare the provider name, account reference, opening information, balance, payment status and dates with statements and correspondence. A report may show a balance as at a particular reporting date, while a payment made later may not yet appear. Request an updated statement from the credit provider if the figures do not reconcile.

Understand payment history

Payment-profile information may show how an account was reported over time. Compare the months shown with bank statements, receipts and provider statements. Be precise: if March is wrong but April is correct, identify March rather than claiming the entire account history is false. Keep the report date because information can change after a bureau receives an update.

Understand adverse information

Adverse classifications can relate to consumer behaviour, such as delinquent, default or slow paying, or enforcement action, such as handover for collection or legal action. A label does not tell the complete story. Ask who supplied the information, what event it refers to, when it was reported and what supporting records exist.

Check enquiries and legal records

Look at the organisation and date for each enquiry. If you do not recognise one, check whether it could relate to a quotation, application or another interaction before disputing it. For a judgment or court-related entry, compare the report with the actual court order and any later order. Court issues can have consequences beyond credit reporting, so obtain qualified legal advice where needed.

Why bureau reports may differ

Credit bureaus may receive data from different sources and update at different times. Their scoring models may also differ. Therefore, scores are not necessarily identical and one bureau's report should not be treated as a complete view of every bureau's records. Keep a dated copy of each report you inspect.

If you find an error

  1. Save the dated report.
  2. Identify the exact entry and disputed field.
  3. Collect evidence that directly proves the issue.
  4. Submit a dispute through the bureau's official channel.
  5. Keep the reference number, response and all supporting documents.
Important: Negative information is not automatically inaccurate. A dispute should identify a genuine factual or reporting issue, not simply a result you dislike.
